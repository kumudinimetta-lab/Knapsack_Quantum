Abstract
Quantum branch-and-bound frameworks use reversible bounding predicates,
whose implementation costs can offset reductions in oracle queries. We study
the linear-programming relaxation of the 0/1 knapsack problem under a fixed
efficiency-ordered prefix branching rule. A classically certified finite bank of
rational affine bounds preserves every integer-threshold pruning decision with-
out reproducing the fractional bound itself. We derive coverage certificates,
minimum-cardinality constructions on a density grid and over nonnegative ratio-
nal multipliers, and a sufficient construction that avoids capacity-reachability
dynamic programming. A counterexample family shows that a linear number
of multipliers can still be necessary. The reversible construction uses constant-
coefficient arithmetic, explicit register bounds, and compute–copy–uncompute
cleanup. Exact arithmetic checks on 700 original instances and their 700 reduced
cores cover 5,204,456 reachable states with zero mismatches in 20,817,824
bank-floor comparisons. Completed small-circuit basis tests report zero gate
mismatches in 1,120 Fourier-arithmetic basis columns and 2,216 Boolean gate
columns; these tests do not constitute large-instance circuit simulation. On
105 fixed-depth instances, derived logical resources relative to a root-aware
fractional-scan reconstruction have median bank-to-control ratios of 0.4981 for
controlled-NOT gates, 0.4908 for active qubits, and 1.0720 for depth. The results
establish scoped predicate preservation and a gate-count–depth tradeoff, not
hardware advantage or end-to-end quantum speedup.
Keywords: quantum computing, knapsack problem, reversible arithmetic, linear
programming, branch-and-bound, quantum oracles

1 Introduction
The 0/1 knapsack problem asks for a subset of maximum total value whose total
weight does not exceed a given capacity. Linear-programming (LP) bounds and classi-
cal core methods exploit structure beyond enumeration [1–3]. Quantum backtracking
and branch-and-bound (B&B) frameworks motivate a different implementation ques-
tion: how much reversible computation is required to decide that a partial assignment
cannot reach an integer objective target? Their query guarantees assume access to the
required oracles [4, 5]; an oracle query is not a unit of physical runtime.
The distinction between a numerical bound and its pruning predicate is useful here.
For integer profits and an integer target, two fractional upper bounds produce identical
pruning decisions whenever their floors agree. A reversible oracle can therefore preserve
the decision without returning the fractional LP value. The difficulty is to establish
this equality at every allowed prefix, rather than selecting a few multipliers that merely
give valid but weaker bounds.
We study a static, instance-dependent bank of nonnegative rational multipliers
under a fixed nonincreasing exact-efficiency order. Our construction links all-prefix
coverage to interval stabbing and compiles the resulting bank into a clean reversible
predicate. Affine Lagrangian bounds, minimization over multiplier sets, integer round-
ing, interval stabbing, and reversible arithmetic are established techniques [6–9]. The
contribution is a construction combining these techniques with coverage certificates
for the integer LP predicate and a matched reversible-resource comparison.
The paper makes four concrete contributions. First, it specifies the exact prefix and
integer-threshold contract and derives finite-grid and rational-bank coverage certifi-
cates. Second, it gives a sufficient construction without capacity-reachability dynamic
programming and a family requiring linearly many multipliers. Third, it describes a
reversible constant-coefficient implementation with bounded unsigned intermediates
2

and cleaned workspace. Fourth, it evaluates arithmetic coverage, small-circuit behav-
ior, and logical resources separately. The comparison with a root-aware scan control
shows lower gate counts and qubit requirements alongside increased median depth.
Sections 3–4 develop the predicate and coverage results. Section 5 describes the
circuit and comparison control. Sections 6 and 7 give the evaluation protocol and
findings, and Section 8 discusses their scope.
2 Literature Review
Classical exact methods. Fractional LP bounds have a long history in discrete
optimization [1]. The Horowitz–Sahni meet-in-the-middle (MitM) algorithm splits
the items into two halves and combines partial solutions, providing a classical
O(2n/2) arithmetic-operation baseline [10]. Kellerer, Pferschy, and Pisinger consolidate
dynamic programming, greedy bounds, and branch-and-bound (B&B) [2]. The core
concept was introduced by Balas and Zemel [3]; Pisinger subsequently showed why core
difficulty must be assessed across capacities and instance distributions [11]. MINKNAP
uses an expanding-core dynamic program [12], while COMBO combines dynamic pro-
gramming with strong bounds [13]. These methods provide stronger classical context
than brute-force baselines alone. Beier and V¨ocking’s expected-polynomial knapsack
analysis [14] and Dey, Dubey, and Molinaro’s random binary-IP B&B analysis [15]
require their respective distributional assumptions; neither establishes polynomial
search trees for the benchmark classes considered here.
Quantum search and its limits. For an unstructured space of size N containing one
marked item, Grover’s algorithm finds that item with bounded error using O(
√
N)
oracle queries [16]. Amplitude amplification generalizes this principle [17], with opti-
mization variants via pure adaptive search [18] and D¨urr–Høyer minimum finding
[19]. Treating the 2n binary assignments as an unstructured objective table gives
an O(2n/2)-query minimum-finding baseline at constant success probability, before

accounting for oracle cost [19]. Bennett et al.’s black-box lower bounds [20] concern
unstructured oracle access; they do not establish an unrestricted lower bound for
structured knapsack algorithms or equate query complexity with runtime.
Subset-sum algorithms and memory assumptions. Closely related subset-sum work
includes the representation technique of Becker, Coron, and Joux [21] and the quan-
tum algorithms of Bernstein et al. [22] and Helm–May [23]. Their input models and,
where used, heuristic assumptions must be distinguished from worst-case optimization
with separate weights and values. Bonnetain et al. identify a problem with a walk-
update heuristic and develop alternative algorithms and memory tradeoffs [24]; this is
not a rejection of every quantum subset-sum algorithm. Jaques and Rattew distinguish
quantum random-access memory (QRAM) architectures and their resource assump-
tions [25]. Such costs require explicit accounting, rather than a blanket assertion that
quantum-addressable memory is impossible.
Gate-model knapsack and hybrid optimization. Hou and Perkowski give gate-model
bounded-knapsack circuits that test complete candidate assignments [26]. Hamitouche
et al. combine Grover-based feasibility and threshold tests with a separately com-
puted classical upper bound, for which a fractional-knapsack bound is one option
[27]. Wilkening et al. present a quantum tree generator (QTG) with amplitude ampli-
fication; separately, their quantum B&B (QBnB) benchmarks use fractional-greedy
bounds [28]. Their released R1 software and experiment data provide a version-specific
implementation reference [29]. These works make quantum knapsack arithmetic and
the use of fractional bounds prior art. Christiansen et al. combine feasible-solution
state preparation with the Grover-mixer quantum approximate optimization algo-
rithm (QAOA) [30], within the variational framework introduced by Farhi et al. [31].
Christiansen et al. establish arbitrarily high optimal-solution sampling probability at
4

sufficiently large depth with suitable parameters [30]; this does not establish a practi-
cal finite-depth advantage. Grover adaptive search for constrained polynomial binary
optimization [32] is a distinct search approach, rather than a variational algorithm.
LP and Lagrangian bounds in quantum methods. Sanavio et al. combine classi-
cal B&B with quantum annealing for integer linear problems [33]. Bo˙zejko et al. use
Lagrangian multiplier bounds in a D-Wave knapsack B&B framework, including mini-
mization over a multiplier set and integer rounding in pruning [6]. Their large-instance
device experiments evaluate bounds at the root, rather than executing the full search
tree on the quantum processing unit (QPU). Gabbassov et al. study Lagrangian duality
for constrained quantum optimization and compare it with quadratic unconstrained
binary optimization (QUBO) reformulations [34]. Thus, neither Lagrangian relaxation
nor taking an integer floor of a valid upper bound is itself a new contribution.
Query frameworks and reversible implementation. Montanaro’s quantum back-
tracking [4] and quantum B&B [5] give query reductions under specified access to tree
and bounding oracles. These frameworks do not make the cost of implementing an
LP oracle free. Martiel and Remaud study concrete circuit implementation of quan-
tum backtracking [35]. Reversible arithmetic includes the networks of Vedral, Barenco,
and Ekert [8], Draper’s Fourier-basis addition [9], and Cuccaro et al.’s ripple-carry
addition [36]; elementary controlled-gate decompositions are treated by Barenco et al.
[37]. Thapliyal et al.’s reversible integer divider [38] is a useful arithmetic comparison,
but the constant-coefficient oracle construction considered here does not execute that
divider. Minimum interval stabbing is also an established combinatorial problem [7],
so any contribution using it must concern the reduction and its endpoint conditions,
rather than the greedy algorithm itself. Preskill’s discussion of noise and depth con-
straints [39] motivates separating logical circuit resources from device performance.
Qiskit [40] supplies software tools; its use alone is not evidence of execution on a
quantum processor.

Closest prior work and our position. The relevant distinction is between computing
a fractional bound, testing a complete assignment, and reversibly deciding whether
the LP relaxation of a partial prefix excludes an integer target. The construction
under study targets the last task: for a fixed exact-efficiency prefix order, a classically
certified bank of affine bounds is compiled into a reversible predicate that preserves the
integer-threshold LP-pruning decisions. This scope requires positive integer weights,
nonnegative integer profits, a coverage certificate, representable integer thresholds, a
separate capacity check, and clean initial workspace. It does not imply reproduction
of the numerical fractional LP value, support for arbitrary branching orders, or an
implementation of the complete quantum walk. Circuit comparisons reconstructed
from published fractional-greedy primitives [28, 29] must therefore account for both
predicate semantics and resource costs, and be identified as reconstructions rather
than released LP-oracle circuits. Table 1 summarizes the roles of the closest approaches
without asserting historical priority or a hardware advantage.

3.1 Core preprocessing and interpretation
The oracle can be applied to an original instance or to a separately justified reduced
core. In the core panel used below, a feasible greedy solution supplies an integer lower
bound L. A variable is fixed only when the opposite assignment is infeasible or its
LP bound is strictly below L. Every feasible solution with value at least L must then
respect that fixing. Applying such individually necessary fixings together preserves all
such solutions. Subtracting fixed-in weight and profit produces the residual instance;
its threshold is shifted by the fixed-in profit. The bank is rebuilt for this residual
instance and its own efficiency order. This argument does not preserve arbitrary low-
threshold trees of the original instance. Exact reference optima are used only to check
the transformations, not to select bank entries.

Proof Collect all finite endpoints, including zero. Choose a rational representative between

constant within each such cell. A point at zero can be moved a sufficiently small positive
distance without losing membership in any of the finitely many intervals containing it. Any
other included endpoint can likewise be moved slightly to its right: every finite upper endpoint
is excluded. Thus cell representatives suffice. Process intervals by increasing upper endpoint.
If the next interval is not already hit, select a representative immediately below its upper
endpoint; for an unbounded interval use a rational point above the greatest finite endpoint.
For a finite upper endpoint u, the selected representative lies below u and above every lower
endpoint strictly below u. Every remaining interval hit by a point in the earliest-ending
interval has lower endpoint below u and upper endpoint at least u, so this replacement
preserves its coverage. This exchange establishes greedy optimality. Equivalently, the intervals
triggering new points are pairwise disjoint, giving a lower bound equal to the number selected.

4.4 Preprocessing cost and a limitation
Reachability enumeration has up to (n+1)(W +1) states. This is pseudopolynomial in
numerical capacity and can be expensive compared with classical optimization itself.
The grid compiler computes coverage intervals on those states. The rational compiler
additionally intersects up to n −d + 1 affine constraints per state. Preprocessing costs
must therefore be included in any end-to-end performance assessment. If a compilation
budget is exceeded, the constructors return the root-tail bank; its coverage is preserved,
but minimality is not certified.

of density 1, is last. For each i = k + 1, . . . , m, set the first i −k −1 decisions to zero. The
greedy suffix fills k main items and half of item i. Its value is half-integral. Immediately below

First compute the prefix weight and its capacity-rejection flag. For each multiplier,
compute the loss accumulator, compare (13) into a fresh flag, and reverse the accumu-

preserves the input registers. Theorem 2 supplies equivalence to the LP only when
the supplied bank is certified. An arbitrary bank circuit correctly evaluating its own
bound is not automatically an LP-equivalent circuit.

them into a quantum search requires coherent node handling and tree reflections, which
are outside this implementation. An extension to superpositions of depths would also
require depth-controlled prefix arithmetic; its synthesis costs are not included here.

5.3 Root-aware fractional-scan control

6.1 Inputs and evidence categories
The main arithmetic panel contains 700 instances, and the core panel contains their

6.3 Gate checks and resource accounting
Small-circuit tests use independently computed primal LP answers, both target-bit

8 Discussion and limitations
A certified bank preserves the LP rejection decision at integer targets while allowing
its fractional value to differ. This permits a constant-coefficient circuit that avoids
dynamically finding a fractional split. Coverage certificates are required because a
merely valid Lagrangian bound can prune less than the LP. The root-tail construction
is a safe baseline; smaller banks require additional coverage reasoning.
22

These results support a compiler for the stated prefix predicate and quantify its
reversible resource tradeoff. Lagrangian multiplier sets and integer rounding already
occur in prior work [6], and fractional-greedy bounds already occur in quantum
knapsack B&B comparisons [28]. The contribution concerns the all-prefix coverage con-
struction and its implementation; minimum bank cardinality does not imply minimum
gate count.
The circuit contract requires positive integer weights, nonnegative integer prof-
its, a fixed efficiency-ordered prefix, representable integer thresholds, and clean initial
workspace. It returns no numerical LP value, integrality flag, completion witness, or
LP-selected branching variable. Those outputs would require additional computation.
Arithmetic coverage and gate-level simulation establish different properties; the ini-
tialization discrepancy demonstrates the need for preparation-only and independent
dense-reference checks.
The resource comparison assumes ideal arbitrary-angle gates and all-to-all con-
nectivity. Hardware costs would additionally depend on routing, rotation synthesis,
and error management. A complete search implementation would still need coherent
node handling, branching and marking operations, reflections, error allocation, wit-
ness recovery, and preprocessing. Quantum-query frameworks [4, 5] provide context,
but do not supply the missing implementation costs. The reported oracle resource
reductions therefore do not establish an end-to-end runtime advantage.
The resource tradeoff depends on the arithmetic layout and compilation model.
Alternative implementations may change the relative gate counts, qubit requirements,
and depth.
9 Conclusion
A certified static multiplier bank can preserve integer-threshold LP-pruning decisions
for fixed efficiency-ordered knapsack prefixes. Coverage reduces to interval constraints,
23

and the predicate can be expressed through bounded constant-coefficient arithmetic
with reversible cleanup. Exact arithmetic checks cover the original and reduced-
core instances, and separate small-circuit simulations test the reversible predicate.
The matched root-aware comparison finds lower CX counts and qubit requirements
throughout its 105-input cohort, together with increased depth on most inputs. These
findings support a specific oracle construction and an explicit resource tradeoff; they
do not establish physical or end-to-end quantum advantage.

A Counterexamples delimiting the contract
These exact arithmetic examples falsify stronger statements, not the stated integer-
prefix contract. With w = (1, 1), v = (1, 1), W = 1, and bank {1/2}, the root LP is
1 while the bank bound is 3/2. Their floors agree, but the decisions differ at rational
target 5/4.
For nonprefix branching, take w = (10, 9), v = (100, 1), W = 15. The root-tail
grid is {0, 1/9}. Fixing the lower-density item in first leaves the high-density item
24

with capacity 6: the total LP bound is 61, while the tail-bank bound is 905/9. They
disagree at integer target 62. Prefix order is therefore a substantive assumption.
For tied groups, take w = (2, 2, 3), v = (1, 1, 1), W = 1. The LP is 1/2, but λ = 1/3
gives bound 1. Its distance from the split density 1/2 is 1/6. A radius using one tied
item’s weight would admit it (1/6 < 1/4), whereas the required group-weight radius
is 1/8. For strictness, w = v = (1) and W = 0 give LP 0; λ = 0 at distance 1 from
density 1 gives bound 1, so the nonstrict radius fails at target 1.
Negative multipliers are unsafe: with w = v = (1) and W = 2, λ = −1 gives 0 below
the LP value 1. Integer weight normalization also need not preserve the fractional
LP: w = (2), v = (3), W = 1 has LP 3/2, whereas division of weight by 2 and
flooring capacity gives LP 0. Equal integer feasible sets do not imply equal LP-pruning
predicates.
